# Evidence-based instructional design for sales-force training (input for a mobile "Learning Roadmap" for life-insurance agents)

**Method note (read first).** All findings below come from web-search extracts (titles, abstracts, snippets) collected 2026-11-24. No full text was fetched in this environment, so every number should be treated as "as reported in the search extract of the linked page". Where the extract was a secondary summary of a primary paper, that is stated. Evidence labels:

- **[STRONG]** = peer-reviewed meta-analysis / systematic review / randomized field experiment.
- **[MODERATE]** = single peer-reviewed study, professional-body survey (ATD, LIMRA/LOMA), or primary paper seen only through a secondary summary.
- **[PRACTITIONER]** = vendor or consultancy research, model/framework definitions, correlational industry surveys.
- **[UNVERIFIED]** = number circulating without a traceable primary source; do not cite as fact.

## 1. Training evaluation models: Kirkpatrick, New World Kirkpatrick, Phillips ROI — and how to map them to observable evidence

### Takeaway
Kirkpatrick's four levels (Reaction, Learning, Behaviour, Results) and Phillips' fifth level (ROI) are useful classification schemes for evidence, not validated causal chains: meta-analytic work finds little correlation between reaction data and the higher levels. The New World Kirkpatrick additions — "required drivers" at Level 3 and "leading indicators" at Level 4 — map most directly onto a roadmap milestone that mixes learning, practice, field application and results.

### Cited Findings
- [PRACTITIONER – framework definition] New World Kirkpatrick Model attaches **required drivers** to Level 3: "processes and systems that reinforce, monitor, encourage, and reward performance of critical behaviors on the job" (examples given: job aids, coaching, work review, pay-for-performance, recognition). — [Kirkpatrick Partners, Introduction to the New World Kirkpatrick Model (PDF)](https://kirkpatrickpartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Introduction-to-The-New-World-Kirkpatrick®-Model.pdf)
- [PRACTITIONER – framework definition] **Leading indicators** (Level 4) are "short-term observations and measurements that suggest that critical behaviors are on track to create a positive impact on the desired results"; sales volume, customer satisfaction and employee engagement are cited examples. — [Kirkpatrick Partners intro, mirrored copy](https://www.cpedv.org/sites/main/files/file-attachments/introduction_to_the_kirkpatrick_new_world_model_-eval_002.pdf); background in [ATD, "Updating the Four Levels for the New World"](https://www.td.org/insights/updating-the-four-levels-for-the-new-world)
- [PRACTITIONER – framework definition] A US-government adaptation of the model for agencies exists as a field guide (OPM, 2011), useful as a neutral, non-vendor description of the levels. — [OPM Field Guide to Training Evaluation](https://www.opm.gov/WIKI/uploads/docs/Wiki/OPM/training/Field%20Guide%20to%20Training%20Evaluation_6-8-2011-FINAL.pdf)
- [PRACTITIONER – framework definition] Phillips adds **Level 5, ROI**: the monetary value of programme benefits compared with total programme cost; isolating the effect of training from other influences is named as a key challenge. Level 5 belongs to Phillips' variant, not to Kirkpatrick's own four-level model. — [Training Industry, Phillips ROI Methodology](https://trainingindustry.com/wiki/measurement-and-analytics/phillips-roi-methodology/); [Whatfix summary (vendor blog)](https://whatfix.com/blog/phillips-roi-model/)
- [STRONG] Alliger, Tannenbaum, Bennett, Traver & Shotland (1997), *Personnel Psychology* 50, 341–358: meta-analysis organised on Kirkpatrick's levels; secondary sources report it found little evidence of substantial correlations between measures at different outcome levels, and it split reactions into **affective** vs **utility** reactions (recommending utility-judgement items on reaction forms). — [Alliger et al. 1997 (full-text mirror)](https://typeset.io/pdf/a-meta-analysis-of-the-relations-among-training-criteria-fmpsav5yk0.pdf); [DTIC technical report version](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA368508.pdf)
- [MODERATE – seen via secondary summary] Bates (2004) critique of Kirkpatrick: challenges the assumption of causality between levels and the assumption that information becomes more important as levels rise. — [Hiroshima University paper summarising Bates](https://ir.lib.hiroshima-u.ac.jp/34634/files/40948)
- [MODERATE] A sales-specific five-level evaluation framework (learning, behaviour, operational results, financial value) has been proposed in the *Journal of Selling & Major Account Management*; its authors note that minimal empirical research has been published on evaluating sales training. — [WKU Journal of Selling paper](https://www.wku.edu/jos/documents/issues/v13n1/13-01-02.pdf)
- [MODERATE] Singh, Manrai & Manrai (2015) reviewed 56 sales-training articles (1985–2014) and state that no comprehensive review of sales-training research existed before theirs. — [Sales training: a state of the art](https://www.elsevier.es/en-revista-journal-economics-finance-administrative-science-352-articulo-sales-training-a-state-art-S2077188615000025)

### Inferences
- A roadmap milestone with four components maps cleanly onto the evidence levels: **Learn** → Level 2 (quiz / retrieval score), **Practice** → Level 2 skill demonstration (scored role-play), **Field application** → Level 3 (observed critical behaviours, activity logs), **Business results** → Level 4 leading indicators (appointments, fact-finds, cases submitted, persistency) rather than lagging annual premium alone.
- Because reaction scores do not predict learning or behaviour (Alliger et al.), **satisfaction/"smile-sheet" ratings should never count toward milestone completion**; if collected, ask utility questions ("will you use this with a client this week?").
- Because the level-to-level causal chain is assumed, not proven (Bates), each milestone should **verify each level independently** rather than inferring behaviour from a passed quiz or inferring skill from a sale. A sale without the observed behaviour, or the behaviour without a sale, are both informative.
- Manager coaching, job aids and recognition are, in New World Kirkpatrick terms, "required drivers" — i.e. part of the roadmap's design, not external to it.
- Phillips ROI is best reserved for programme-level evaluation (cohort vs cohort); it is not a per-agent completion criterion, because isolating training effects at individual level is not credible.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed source was found that empirically validates how sales organisations in practice map Kirkpatrick levels to evidence; the mapping above is inference from framework definitions.
- Bates (2004) original text and the exact wording of its "three limitations" were not retrieved.
- Phillips' own publications (ROI Institute) were not retrieved; the ROI formula and the recommended isolation techniques are not quoted here for that reason.

## 2. Competency-/mastery-based vs time-based progression; proficiency levels and their assessment

### Takeaway
Mastery-based progression has solid evidence for learning outcomes in education (classic meta-analysis reports roughly half a standard deviation gain), while direct head-to-head evidence that competency-based beats time-based progression in professional training is still thin. Proficiency scales such as Dreyfus are widely used descriptive conventions, not empirically validated stage models; observed practice with feedback (behaviour modelling) is the best-evidenced assessment-and-training format.

### Cited Findings
- [STRONG – effect size seen via abstract summary] Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990), *Review of Educational Research* 60(2): meta-analysis of 108 controlled evaluations; mastery learning programmes raised examination performance by an average of about 0.52 SD. Context is school and college, not workplace. — [Effectiveness of Mastery Learning Programs: A Meta-Analysis](https://typeset.io/pdf/effectiveness-of-mastery-learning-programs-a-meta-analysis-45bgwh1byb.pdf)
- [MODERATE] Competency-based education in health professions emerged as an outcomes-oriented alternative to time-based credentialing where "competence was assumed after fixed training durations"; a 2010 systematic review of definitions found CBE is described in several inconsistent ways. — [Frank et al. 2010, Competency-based medical education: theory to practice](https://www.cpd.utoronto.ca/summit/files/2014/10/5600-Frank-J-et-al-Competency-based-medical-education_theory-to-practice.pdf)
- [MODERATE] A 2024 systematized review on evaluating competency-based medical curricula notes few published articles give a comprehensive overview of available evidence — i.e. outcome evidence for CBME vs time-based is still limited. — [BMC Medical Education 2024](https://bmcmededuc.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12909-024-05609-6.pdf)
- [PRACTITIONER – descriptive model] Dreyfus model: five stages — novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, expert. One empirical study found learners showing "novice" and "advanced beginner" characteristics simultaneously, i.e. the stages are not cleanly separable. — [PMC7355480](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7355480)
- [STRONG] Taylor, Russ-Eft & Chan (2005), *Journal of Applied Psychology* 90, 692–709: meta-analysis of behaviour modelling training (model the behaviour → trainee practice → feedback and social reinforcement); 117 studies, 4,200+ participants per a practitioner summary. Effects on knowledge and skills are large; effects on attitudes and on-the-job behaviour are modest. Transfer improves when learners are reinforced or sanctioned for using the skills on the job, and when key learning points are presented as underlying rules. — [ScienceForWork summary](https://scienceforwork.com/blog/training-make-change-stick/); [QIC-WD umbrella summary](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Behavior%20Modeling%20Training%20112421b.pdf)
- [STRONG] Kluger & DeNisi (1996), *Psychological Bulletin* 119, 254–284: 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations; feedback interventions improved performance on average (d = .41) but **more than one-third decreased performance**. Feedback is less effective as attention shifts from the task toward the self. — [Kluger & DeNisi abstract (author's site)](https://scholars.huji.ac.il/node/1802); [full text mirror](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Marking%20and%20Feedback/The%20effects%20of%20feedback%20interventions.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Gate progression on demonstrated performance, not elapsed days or content "viewed".** Time should appear only as a guard-rail (minimum spacing between attempts; expected pace as a nudge), consistent with the mastery-learning evidence. The caveat is that the 0.52 SD figure comes from classroom education; transfer to adult sales training is plausible but not directly demonstrated.
- A **3- or 4-level proficiency scale with behavioural anchors** (e.g. "not yet / with prompts / independently / can coach others") is a defensible simplification; do not present Dreyfus-style labels as scientifically validated stages. Agents can be at different levels on different skills at once, so rate **per skill**, not per person.
- **Role-play certification should follow the behaviour-modelling sequence**: show a model call → agent practises → rubric-based feedback → manager reinforcement in the field. Expect it to move skill substantially and job behaviour only modestly unless on-the-job reinforcement exists.
- Rubrics should be **task-focused** (what was said/done against observable criteria), not person-focused (rankings, "you are a weak closer"), since Kluger & DeNisi show self-directed feedback can lower performance. Leaderboards and public pass/fail badges are a design risk on this basis.
- Completion verification hierarchy (inference): system-captured data (policy/CRM records) > third-party observation with rubric (manager, trainer) > artefact upload (recorded pitch, completed fact-find) > self-report tick-box. Self-report alone should not close a field-application or results component.

### Gaps
- No systematic review directly comparing outcomes of competency-based vs time-based progression in sales or insurance was found.
- No peer-reviewed evidence on reliability (inter-rater agreement) of sales role-play certification or manager skill-check rubrics was retrieved; rubric design guidance here is inference.
- The primary Dreyfus & Dreyfus report and dedicated critiques of it were not retrieved; no source was found for a specific "4-level scale" being superior to other scale lengths.
- McGaghie et al. (mastery learning with deliberate practice in simulation) was searched but not retrieved.
- Taylor et al. (2005) exact effect sizes were not confirmed against the primary paper (a garbled secondary snippet gave d = 0.27 for job behaviour — treat as unverified).

## 3. Learning-science findings relevant to mobile sales training

### Takeaway
Spaced practice and retrieval practice (testing) are the two best-supported learning techniques and suit mobile delivery well; microlearning evidence is positive but scattered and methodologically weaker; the "70-20-10" ratio has no solid empirical basis and should be used only as a reminder that most development happens on the job, not as a quantitative allocation rule.

### Cited Findings
**Spacing and retrieval**
- [STRONG] Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3), 354–380: review and quantitative synthesis of distributed practice in verbal recall — 839 assessments in 317 experiments from 184 articles. (No single pooled effect size was visible in the extract.) — [PubMed 16719566](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)
- [STRONG – effect size via secondary source] Rowland (2014), *Psychological Bulletin* 140(6), 1432–1463: meta-analysis of testing vs restudy; secondary sources report d ≈ 0.50, larger for recall than recognition tests; a broader review puts testing-effect meta-analytic estimates at 0.50–0.61. — [PubMed 25150680](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/); [Frontiers in Education 2021 meta-review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/full)
- [STRONG] Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4–58: of 10 learning techniques reviewed, only **practice testing** and **distributed practice** received the highest utility rating; rereading and highlighting were rated low. — [PubMed 26173288](https://www.pubmed.gov/26173288/); [AFT summary by Dunlosky](https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky)
- [MODERATE – RCTs in adult professionals; author has a commercial interest] Kerfoot and colleagues: randomized trials of online "spaced education" (short spaced questions) with clinicians showed better knowledge acquisition and retention than single "bolus" delivery (2009, *Annals of Surgery*), and online spaced education after a live course increased self-reported clinical behaviours (2011). Kerfoot holds equity in Spaced Education Inc. (Qstream). — [Kerfoot 2009 PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2009-kerfoot-2.pdf); [PubMed 21671276](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21671276/); [2024 study in practising physicians](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39250798/)

**Forgetting curve**
- [MODERATE] Murre & Dros (2015), *PLoS ONE*: replicated Ebbinghaus' forgetting curve using the savings method — but with **one subject**, 70 hours of learning nonsense-syllable lists, retention intervals 20 min to 31 days; Ebbinghaus' original also had a single subject. — [PMC4492928](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4492928/)

**Microlearning**
- [MODERATE] Monib et al. (2024/2025), *Heliyon*, systematic review: reviewed studies indicate a positive impact of microlearning on learning outcomes, but prior results are "scattered and mixed". — [PMC11774797](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11774797/)
- [MODERATE] De Gagne et al. (2019), scoping review of microlearning in health-professions education — maps outcomes, gives no pooled effect. — [PMC6683654](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6683654)
- [MODERATE] A higher-education meta-analysis (Senadheera et al.) pooled only 5 studies of microlearning vs conventional learning; result direction/size not visible in the extract. — [Search extract, source list in PubMed 39882484 context](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39882484/)

**Deliberate practice and feedback**
- [STRONG] Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014), *Psychological Science*: meta-analysis; deliberate practice explained 26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and **less than 1% in professions**. — [PubMed 24986855](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24986855/)
- [MODERATE] Counter-critique: a 2019 review argues Macnamara et al. used a broader definition than Ericsson's (which requires individualised tasks set by a teacher with immediate feedback), so the result reflects "structured practice", not deliberate practice. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2019](https://frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full)
- [STRONG] Feedback interventions: average d = .41 but over one-third harmful (Kluger & DeNisi 1996, see section 2). — [Kluger & DeNisi](https://scholars.huji.ac.il/node/1802)

**Training transfer (general)**
- [STRONG] Salas, Tannenbaum, Kraiger & Smith-Jentsch (2012), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 13(2), 74–101: training works, and what happens **before** (needs analysis, learning climate) and **after** (transfer support, evaluation) matters as much as the training event itself; encountering errors during training helps prepare trainees for real application. — [APS journal page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/journals/pspi/training-and-development.html); [PubMed 26173283](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173283/)
- [STRONG] Blume, Ford, Baldwin & Huang (2010), *Journal of Management* 36(4), 1065–1105: meta-analysis of 89 studies on predictors of transfer (trainee characteristics, work environment, interventions). Secondary summary: positive transfer climate is associated with transfer; post-training goal-setting interventions alone were not significant. — [Blume et al. listing](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/811150895751364610); [QIC-WD summary](https://qic-wd.org/node/776)

**70-20-10**
- [MODERATE – critique from professional body] The ratio is traced to Center for Creative Leadership research in which 191 successful executives recalled key career events; the phrase "70-20-10" never appears in *Lessons of Experience* (1988); Lombardo & Eichinger popularised the split in a 1996 planner. ATD calls it a conceptual model "based on retrospective musings by executives" and essentially untestable. — [ATD, "70-20-10: Where Is the Evidence?"](https://www.td.org/insights/70-20-10-where-is-the-evidence)
- [MODERATE] A University of Twente piece (2026) states there is no solid scientific evidence for the ratio and cites an empirical study reporting a very different split (16% experience, 44% from others, 30% formal training). — [University of Twente, Rethinking the 70-20-10 Rule](https://www.utwente.nl/en/learning-teaching/news-events/2026/4/871038/rethinking-the-70-20-10-rule)

### Inferences
- **Build spaced retrieval into the roadmap as a first-class mechanic**: short recall questions (free recall / scenario response preferred over recognition-only multiple choice, per Rowland) reappearing days and weeks after a module, including content from earlier milestones. A milestone's "Learn" component should close on a **delayed** retrieval check, not on an end-of-video quiz taken immediately.
- Do **not quote specific forgetting percentages** ("people forget X% in Y days") in the product or pitch: the replicated curve is one subject memorising nonsense syllables; rates for meaningful sales knowledge are not established by it. The directional claim (unreinforced knowledge decays; spaced reinforcement counteracts it) is well supported.
- Microlearning is a reasonable **delivery format** for knowledge and for spaced retrieval on mobile, but the evidence does not support it as sufficient for complex skills such as needs-based selling conversations; these need practice with feedback (section 2).
- The "<1% in professions" finding warns against rewarding **volume of practice** (number of role-plays logged). Reward practice that is specific, at the edge of current ability, and followed by task-focused feedback.
- Because transfer depends on the work environment (Salas; Blume), a mobile app alone is unlikely to change field behaviour: the roadmap needs explicit manager/leader touchpoints and a field-application step in every milestone.
- Use 70-20-10 only as a design heuristic ("most of each milestone should be doing, with others, in the field"); do not allocate hours or points by 70/20/10 or claim it is research-proven.

### Gaps
- No controlled study of spaced/retrieval practice or microlearning **specifically in sales or insurance agents** was found; the evidence is from students and clinicians.
- Optimal spacing intervals for a multi-month roadmap could not be sourced from the extracts (Cepeda et al. 2008 "temporal ridgeline" paper not retrieved).
- The original CCL publication and Lombardo & Eichinger (1996) were not retrieved; origin details rely on ATD's account. The Twente-cited empirical study and Kajewski & Madsen (2012) were not retrieved directly.
- Widely repeated claims such as "87% of sales training is forgotten within a month" (attributed variously to Xerox/Huthwaite) could not be traced to any primary source — **[UNVERIFIED]**; no source found confirming or debunking.
- Baldwin & Ford (1988) original transfer model was not retrieved directly (covered via Blume et al. 2010).

## 4. Sales onboarding benchmarks (ramp time; effect of structured onboarding and coaching)

### Takeaway
Reliable, independently verifiable benchmarks for ramp time are scarce: nearly all published numbers come from vendor or consultancy surveys and are correlational. The most defensible statements are qualitative — longer, structured onboarding and coaching are associated with better performance — plus insurance-industry retention figures showing that most new agents leave within four years.

### Cited Findings
**General B2B sales (mostly US, mostly correlational surveys)**
- [MODERATE – professional-body survey] ATD sales-onboarding research: organisations whose sales onboarding lasts **six months or longer** were significantly more likely to be top performers; faster time to productivity was the most cited benefit. — [ATD press release](https://www.td.org/content/press-release/employee-productivity-increases-with-sales-onboarding); [ATD whitepaper, Sales Onboarding: Launching Productivity](https://www.td.org/whitepaper/sales-onboarding-launching-productivity)
- [MODERATE – professional-body survey] ATD 2019 State of Sales Training: average spend of $2,326 (median $1,000) per salesperson per year on sales training. — [ATD, 2019 State of Sales Training](https://www.td.org/insights/atd-research-presents-2019-state-of-sales-training)
- [PRACTITIONER – via vendor citation] CSO Insights Sales Enablement Report 2018: onboarding services that meet or exceed expectations are associated with 16.2% better quota attainment (as cited by Mindtickle; primary not checked). Mindtickle pages give inconsistent CSO Insights 2018 quota-attainment figures (54.3% average attainment on one page vs 53% of reps making quota on another). — [Mindtickle](https://www.mindtickle.com/blog/15-stats-about-onboarding-sales-reps-you-cant-afford-to-ignore/); primary report: [CSO Insights 2018 Sales Enablement Report (hosted by Brainshark)](https://www.brainshark.com/sites/default/files/cso-insights-sales-enablement-report-2018_0.pdf)
- [PRACTITIONER – vendor research] RAIN Group: top-performing sellers are 58% more likely to report receiving very/extremely effective sales training; 71% of top performers vs 45% of others rate their training that way; sellers with an effective manager + regular coaching + effective training are 63% more likely to be top performers. Self-reported, correlational, produced by a training vendor. — [RAIN Group, Top-Performing Seller research](https://rainsalestraining.com/sales-research/the-top-performing-seller); [Benchmark report PDF](https://www.rainsalestraining.com/hubfs/PDFs/The_Top-Performing_Seller_Benchmark_Report.pdf)
- [UNVERIFIED – vendor blogs without named primary source] "Onboarding takes an average of 38 days and costs $9,589 per salesperson"; "nine months to competence, 15 months to top performer"; "onboarding cuts ramp time by 50%" (Litmos); "up to 30% ramp reduction", "40% of quota by month 2, 80% by month 4" (Demodesk); "47% faster ramp, 62% higher first-year attainment" (Apollo). — [Litmos](https://www.litmos.com/blog/articles/sales-onboarding-roi); [Demodesk](https://demodesk.com/blog/ramp-up-new-sales-reps-faster); [Apollo](https://www.apollo.io/insights/sales-onboarding)
- [PRACTITIONER – methodology] Ramp can be operationalised as time to 50% and to 100% of prorated quota. — [Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/company-analysis/sales/ramp-up-time-for-new-reps/)

**Life-insurance agents**
- [MODERATE – industry commentary citing LIMRA] US career-agent four-year retention "still hovers around 15%" (2025 commentary); a LIMRA conference slide says average financial-professional retention for agency-building companies has not exceeded 20%. — [ThinkAdvisor 2025](https://www.thinkadvisor.com/amp/2025/07/14/how-to-fix-the-agent-retention-crisis-in-life-insurance/); [LIMRA 2025 Annual Conference, Session 16 slides](https://www.limra.com/globalassets/limra-loma/events-learning-and-networking/conferences/2025/limra-annual-conference/session-16-building-high-performing-sales-teams.pdf)
- [MODERATE – LOMA article, modelled example] Asia: an illustrative four-year agent retention of 9%, with the argument that a structured process could reach about 40%. This is a modelled scenario, not a measured outcome. — [LOMA 2025, Retaining Life Agents in Asia](https://www.loma.org/en/news/marketfacts/2025/retaining-life-agents-in-asia-lessons-for-sustainable-growth)
- [MODERATE] LIMRA slide: first-year results typically show no difference between groups "because new agents are supported through onboarding, coaching, training and leadership"; four-year retention of 40% vs 13% between two groups whose labels were not legible in the extract. — [LIMRA Session 16 slides](https://www.limra.com/globalassets/limra-loma/events-learning-and-networking/conferences/2025/limra-annual-conference/session-16-building-high-performing-sales-teams.pdf)
- [MODERATE – older LIMRA data via trade press] Canada: four-year retention about 30%; 45% for experienced recruits vs 29% for inexperienced. — [Insurance Portal (Canada)](https://insurance-portal.ca/article/retention-rates-frozen/)
- [MODERATE] Multi-line exclusive agents: four-year retention rose from 51% to 55% between 2014 and 2024 for top recruiting companies. — [LOMA 2025, MLEA distribution](https://www.loma.org/en/news/marketfacts/2025/mlea-distribution-recruiting-dips-but-is-the-sky-falling)

### Inferences
- Plan the roadmap horizon for **at least six months, plausibly 12+**, rather than a 30/60/90-day sprint only: the ATD association with ≥6-month programmes and the insurance retention data (differences emerge after year one, attrition continues to year four) both point to sustained support.
- Because benchmark numbers are weak, the programme should **set its own baseline** (time to first policy, time to N policies, 13-month agent retention, persistency) from the company's historical data and compare cohorts; that is also the only credible route to a Phillips-style ROI.
- Any slide or business case should label the CSO Insights/RAIN numbers as vendor survey correlations (organisations that do X also report Y), not as effects of onboarding.
- Agent retention is a legitimate Level-4 result for this roadmap in insurance, alongside production.

### Gaps
- **No Sales Management Association, Gartner or Korn Ferry primary data on ramp time or onboarding effects was retrieved**; nothing from these sources is cited here.
- No authoritative average ramp-time figure for life-insurance agents was found; no LIMRA 13-month retention benchmark was found.
- No Thailand- or ASEAN-specific measured agent retention/ramp data was found (the LOMA Asia figure is illustrative).
- The CSO Insights 2018 primary PDF was located but not read; the 16.2% figure and quota-attainment baselines are unconfirmed against it.
- No controlled (experimental or quasi-experimental) study of structured onboarding's effect on quota attainment was found.

## 5. Manager coaching, joint field work, and peer learning

### Takeaway
Workplace coaching has meta-analytic support for improving performance, and a randomized field experiment in a sales firm shows structured peer knowledge-sharing can lift sales by more than 15% with lasting effect. Sales-specific numbers on coaching frequency/structure, however, come only from vendor surveys, and no rigorous evidence on joint field work in insurance was found.

### Cited Findings
- [STRONG – but scope excludes manager and peer coaching] Jones, Woods & Guillaume (2016), *Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology*: meta-analysis of 17 studies of workplace coaching by internal/external coaches. Overall effect on organisational outcomes δ = 0.36; skill-based δ = 0.28; affective δ = 0.51; individual-level results δ = 1.24. Internal coaches had stronger effects than external ones; **coaching format and duration did not moderate effects**; use of multisource feedback moderated effects. Manager–subordinate and peer coaching were excluded. — [Jones et al. 2016 (Reading repository)](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/74522/); [Aston record](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-workplace-coaching-a-meta-analysis-of-learni/)
- [STRONG] A 2023 meta-analysis of post-2018 research again finds workplace coaching effective for organisational outcomes. — [PMC10597717](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10597717/)
- [STRONG – randomized field experiment in a sales firm] Sandvik, Saouma, Seegert & Stanton (2020), "Workplace Knowledge Flows", *Quarterly Journal of Economics*: salespeople encouraged to seek advice from a **randomly assigned partner during structured meetings** had average sales gains **exceeding 15%**, lasting **at least 20 weeks** after the intervention ended. Paying pairs for joint output gave positive but **transitory** gains. Gains were largest for those paired with high performers. — [NBER w26660](https://nber.org/papers/w26660); [CEPR DP14299](https://cepr.org/publications/dp14299)
- [MODERATE – natural experiment, retail sales] Chan, Li & Pierce (2014), *Marketing Science* 33(4): among department-store cosmetics salespeople, peer-based learning was more important than learning-by-doing for individuals, with no evidence of forgetting. — [Marketing Science 2014 record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:33:y:2014:i:4:p:463-484)

### Inferences
- **Peer pairing is the best-evidenced social mechanic available for the roadmap.** Design it the way the experiment did: a prompt/structure to *ask a specific partner for advice* (ideally a higher performer) at a scheduled meeting. Simply paying or awarding joint bonuses to pairs is less durable. An open chat feed or "community" tab without structure is not what was tested.
- Coaching should be a **required, verifiable component of milestones** (a "required driver"), verified by the manager's own record (rubric + short note) rather than the agent's tick. Since coaching duration/format did not moderate effects in Jones et al., prioritise that structured, feedback-based coaching happens at all, over prescribing session length.
- **Joint field work** is best framed as the Level-3 observation instrument: manager accompanies or reviews a client meeting, scores observable critical behaviours on the same rubric used in role-play, gives task-focused feedback. This is an inference from behaviour-modelling and transfer evidence, not a tested insurance practice.
- Manager capability is a dependency: the design should include a short manager track (how to observe, how to give task-focused feedback), because poorly given feedback can reduce performance (Kluger & DeNisi).
- Present CSO Insights coaching numbers only as correlations from vendor-distributed surveys with differing baselines across years.

### Gaps
- **No rigorous evidence was found on optimal coaching frequency** (e.g. weekly vs monthly) for sales behaviour change; common "X hours of coaching per month" claims were not located in a primary source.
- No peer-reviewed or LIMRA primary evidence on the effect of joint field work in life insurance was retrieved.
- No evidence specific to communities of practice in sales teams (as opposed to structured pairs) was found.
- Meta-analytic evidence specifically on **manager-as-coach** was not found (Jones et al. excluded it).
- CSO Insights primary PDFs were located but not read; definitions of "dynamic" vs "formal" coaching and sample sizes are unconfirmed.
