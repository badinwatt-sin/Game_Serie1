> Evidence tags: **[STRONG]** = meta-analysis, replicated field experiment, or primary regulator text. **[MODERATE]** = single peer-reviewed study, or a strong finding seen only via secondary summary. **[PRACTITIONER]** = vendor / company self-report / commentary (marketing claims are marked as such).

## 1. What does the research say about gamification in corporate training and sales (effects, moderators, novelty/decay)?

### Takeaway
Gamification has small-to-moderate positive average effects on learning, with the cognitive effect the most robust and the motivational and behavioural effects less stable; results depend heavily on context, user and design. Points, levels and leaderboards on their own mainly raise the *quantity* of activity, not intrinsic motivation or quality, and almost all the rigorous evidence comes from education, not from sales-force training.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG]** Sailer & Homner (2020, Educational Psychology Review) meta-analysis: significant small effect on cognitive learning outcomes (g = .49, 95% CI [0.30, 0.69], k = 19, N = 1,686) and on motivational outcomes (g = .36, 95% CI [0.18, 0.54], k = 16, N = 2,246). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w); [Augsburg repository](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)
- **[STRONG]** Same meta-analysis: in the sub-sample of high-rigour studies the cognitive effect held, while effects on motivational and behavioural outcomes were less stable; inclusion of **game fiction (story)** and **social interaction** were significant moderators for behavioural learning outcomes. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)
- **[STRONG]** Bai, Hew & Huang (2020) meta-analysis in education: overall significant positive effect of gamification on learning outcomes (g = 0.504) versus non-gamified instruction; includes a synthesis of 32 qualitative studies. — [UZH reference page](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Bai-et-al.-(2020))
- **[MODERATE]** Hamari, Koivisto & Sarsa (2014) reviewed 24 peer-reviewed empirical studies: gamification "provides positive effects, however, the effects are greatly dependent on the context in which the gamification is being implemented, as well as on the users using it"; caveats named are context, user qualities and possible novelty effects. — [gamification-research.org summary](https://gamification-research.org/2013/09/does-gamification-work-a-look-into-research/); [paper PDF copy](https://www.lescahiersdelinnovation.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/2014-hamari_et_al-does_gamification_work.pdf)
- **[MODERATE, secondary]** A Hamari slide deck summarising the literature states that gamification effects may work for a while due to novelty and then decline, and that removing gamification from an enterprise social network led to a drop in the wanted behaviour; it also flags crowding-out, where intrinsic motives are replaced by "surrogate" motives. — [Hamari slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/how-does-gamification-work-juho-hamari-game-research-lab-university-of-tampere-finland/39911451)
- **[MODERATE]** Koivisto & Hamari (2019, Int. J. of Information Management) is the larger follow-up review of gamification research. — [Tampere University](https://webpages.tuni.fi/gamification/2019/06/24/the-rise-of-motivational-information-systems-a-review-of-gamification-research/)
- **[MODERATE]** Mekler et al. (2017): in their experiment, points, levels and leaderboards "functioned as extrinsic incentives, effective only for promoting performance quantity"; they did not significantly affect competence need satisfaction or intrinsic motivation. — [Mekler et al. PDF](https://glassmanlab.seas.harvard.edu/annotated_works/mekler17.pdf)
- **[MODERATE]** Hanus & Fox (2015), as described in later reviews: students in a gamified course (badges + leaderboard) showed *less* intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and empowerment, and lower final-exam scores, than the non-gamified class. — [review citing Hanus & Fox, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8916940)
- **[MODERATE]** Sailer, Hense, Mayr & Mandl (2017, Computers in Human Behavior): badges, leaderboards and performance graphs positively affected competence need satisfaction and perceived task meaningfulness; avatars, a meaningful story and teammates positively affected social relatedness. — [Augsburg repository](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109059)
- **[MODERATE]** Landers, Bauer & Callan, "Gamification of task performance with leaderboards: A goal setting experiment": a leaderboard was compared against classic goal-setting conditions (do-your-best, easy, difficult, impossible goals) in a brainstorming task; later literature cites it as evidence that leaderboards raise performance. — [U. Minnesota record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/gamification-of-task-performance-with-leaderboards-a-goal-setting/)
- **[MODERATE]** Ho, Liu & Wang, "Fun Shopping" (Information Systems Research, 2023), randomized field experiment at a large Asian shopping mall (consumers, not salespeople): badges and leaderboards raised sales by 21.5% and 22.5% in the treatment period versus 31.7% for coupons, but the gamification effects persisted after treatment while the coupon effect faded. — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/isre.2022.1147)
- **[MODERATE]** Grobelny et al. (2018) field study in a company: narrative gamification had a positive impact on sales performance after two months. — [AMU repository](https://repozytorium.amu.edu.pl/bitstreams/163d1c53-e4bf-47ed-93b7-773d7324ba23/download)
- **[MODERATE]** Su, "Essays on Sales Gamification" (University of Minnesota dissertation, 2024) studies sales contests with leaderboards and random-reward programs and reports clearly negative consequences of certain elements. — [UMN Conservancy](https://conservancy.umn.edu/items/a0be8711-373e-4fd7-a4ff-616e44b414ae)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** Cognizant's practitioner paper on gamification for insurers warns of "badge fatigue" and that many companies failed to align the game with business objectives. — [Cognizant paper](https://de.slideshare.net/cognizant/gamification-for-insurers-a-practitioners-perspective)

### Inferences
- Expect a real but modest lift, concentrated in learning and activity volume. Gamification should be treated as a delivery layer over a sound curriculum, not as the source of the result.
- Story and social interaction were the moderators that mattered for behaviour. A "passport/journey" metaphor (story) plus unit/team features (social) is better supported than bare points-badges-leaderboard.
- Novelty decay is a credible risk over a 24-month horizon; no study found here measured anything close to 24 months. Plan content refreshes and new "chapters" rather than assuming initial engagement persists.
- The Ho et al. result suggests recognition-type mechanics may fade more slowly than monetary coupons, but this is one consumer study and should not be generalised to agents with confidence.

### Gaps
- Sailer & Homner's behavioural effect size was cut off in the extract. My recollection is g ≈ .25, but this was **not verified** in-session.
- Landers publication year appears as both 2015 and 2017 in sources; exact effect sizes not retrieved.
- Hanus & Fox (2015) and the 2014 Hamari paper's own novelty-effect wording were seen only through secondary summaries.
- No meta-analysis specific to corporate sales training or insurance agents was found. Landers' theory papers (e.g. the theory of gamified learning) were not retrieved.
- No study found tracks a gamified workplace programme for 12-24 months.

## 2. Which motivation theories apply (SDT, goal-setting, goal-gradient, endowed progress, overjustification)?

### Takeaway
Four well-evidenced mechanisms support a stamp book: specific challenging goals, visible progress that accelerates effort near a goal, a head start that increases completion, and competence feedback. The main theoretical hazard is that expected, tangible, controlling rewards undermine intrinsic motivation and shift effort toward quantity rather than quality.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG]** Locke & Latham (2002, American Psychologist 57(9), 705-717): summarises 35 years of goal-setting research covering core findings, mechanisms, moderators (including commitment and feedback), and goals as mediators of incentives; specific difficult goals outperform easy or vague "do your best" goals, which lack an external reference point. — [paper PDF copy](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/locke%20pract%20goal%20setting%202002%20am%20psy.pdf); [bibliographic record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/locke-latham-buildingapracticallyusefultheoryofgoalsettingandtaskmotivationa35yearodyssey-2002)
- **[MODERATE, secondary]** For tasks where people are still acquiring skill, a specific high *learning* goal outperforms a "do your best" condition. — [Wikipedia: Goal setting](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goal_setting)
- **[STRONG]** Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng (2006, Journal of Marketing Research 43, 39-58): in a real café "buy ten, get one free" programme, customers bought more frequently the closer they were to the free coffee; a 12-stamp card with 2 pre-filled bonus stamps was completed faster than a plain 10-stamp card (same 10 purchases required); stronger acceleration predicted greater retention and faster re-engagement; habituation, expiry and heterogeneity explanations were ruled out. — [Columbia Business School](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal)
- **[STRONG]** Nunes & Drèze (2006, Journal of Consumer Research 32(4), 504-512), car-wash field experiment: both cards required 8 purchases; a 10-space card with 2 stamps pre-filled was redeemed by 34% of customers versus 19% for an empty 8-space card over nine months, and pre-stamped customers left less time between washes. Moderators: the stated reason for the endowment and the currency in which progress is recorded. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2006i4p504-512.html); [paper PDF](https://msbfile03.usc.edu/digitalmeasures/jnunes/intellcont/Endowed%20Progress%20Effect-1.pdf); [Coglode summary](https://coglode.com/nuggets/endowed-progress-effect)
- **[MODERATE]** Urminsky & Jang (2023) note that goal-gradient effects and time discounting can be confounded in earlier research. — [paper PDF](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Goal%20vs%20Reward%202023.pdf)
- **[STRONG]** Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999, Psychological Bulletin) meta-analysis of 128 experiments: engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent rewards significantly undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36, -0.28); the effect held for tangible rewards. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/)
- **[MODERATE]** The undermining effect is contested: Deci et al. were responding to Cameron & Pierce (1994), who found negative effects limited and avoidable, and a 2023 dissertation meta-analysis found no compelling support for either undermining or enhancement on self-reported interest/enjoyment. — [Cameron & Pierce line of work, Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF03392017); [Univ. of Turku dissertation](https://www.utupub.fi/bitstream/handle/10024/173853/Annales%20E%2099%20Lehtivuori%20DISS.pdf?sequence=1)
- **[STRONG]** Cerasoli et al. (2014) 40-year meta-analysis (183 studies across school, work and physical domains): intrinsic motivation predicted more unique variance in **quality** of performance, incentives were the better predictor of **quantity**. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24491020/)
- **[MODERATE]** SDT mapping from Sailer et al. (2017): badges, leaderboards and performance graphs feed competence; story, avatars and teammates feed relatedness. — [Augsburg repository](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109059)
- **[MODERATE]** Silverman & Barasch (2023, Journal of Consumer Research, seven studies): highlighting an intact streak increases subsequent engagement relative to highlighting a broken one; a broken streak reduces persistence (even when the break had an external cause), the effect is stronger when people blame themselves and weaker when the streak can be repaired, and it fades with time. — [University of Delaware](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman); [CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** Duolingo's blog says its Streak Freeze keeps learners going after a missed day and cites a Penn/UCLA study that some "slack" motivates more than rigid rules; no retention figures are given there. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)

### Inferences
- **Goal structure:** each stamp should be a specific, moderately hard, verifiable target with feedback. For novice agents (months 1-6), learning and behaviour goals (skills demonstrated, fact-finds completed) fit the evidence better than outcome quotas.
- **Endowed progress:** issue the passport with the first stamps already earned for real prior achievements (licence exam passed, onboarding completed). The moderator in Nunes & Drèze implies the head start needs a stated, credible reason; arbitrary free stamps are weaker.
- **Goal gradient:** break 24 months into short "pages" (e.g. per quarter) so that agents are frequently near a completion point; a single 24-month goal leaves most of the period far from any goal. Show progress as "x of y on this page".
- **Crowding-out:** keep stamps informational (recognition of demonstrated competence) rather than cash-equivalent and controlling. Since incentives predict quantity and intrinsic motivation predicts quality, heavy prize-loading of stamps would be expected to push volume at the expense of advice quality.
- **Streaks:** if used, apply them to low-stakes learning habits only, make them repairable, and never notify about a broken streak.

### Gaps
- Locke & Latham's specific statements on proximal sub-goals and on complex tasks were not retrieved; Bandura & Schunk (1981) on proximal goals and Latham & Seijts on proximal goals in complex tasks are from memory and **unverified**.
- The Sharif & Shu "emergency reserves" paper (slack in goals) was not found; only Duolingo's indirect reference to a Penn/UCLA study.
- Descriptions of the Nunes & Drèze procedure differ between secondary sources (how the 300 cards were allocated); the paper itself was not read.
- No source found tests endowed progress or goal-gradient effects in workplace learning or over multi-year horizons; both field studies are short consumer loyalty programmes.

## 3. Digital badges / micro-credentials best practice and "learning passport" examples

### Takeaway
Open Badges 3.0 gives a ready data model for a stamp: a defined achievement with criteria, an issuer who asserts it, attached evidence and alignment to a competency framework, packaged as a verifiable credential. Evidence that badges themselves improve outcomes is almost entirely company or vendor self-report, and "passport" programmes exist in corporate training but publish little outcome data.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG, primary standard]** Open Badges 3.0 is a final 1EdTech specification compatible with the W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model v2.0 (JSON-LD); the certification guide for the final release is dated 12 December 2025 (document version 1.3). — [1EdTech certification guide](https://www.imsglobal.org/spec/ob/v3p0/cert/); [1EdTech Open Badges](https://www.1edtech.org/standards/open-badges)
- **[STRONG, primary standard]** Data model: an **Achievement** defines the accomplishment; an **AchievementCredential** is the awarded, verifiable badge; **Evidence** is supporting information such as a URL to an artifact the learner produced. The credential may express achievement metadata, alignment to standards or competency frameworks, evidence of attainment, and learner and issuer identity. An issuer "makes a claim that a learner has met the criteria of a particular defined Achievement". — [Dublin Core standards listing](https://msi.dublincore.org/standards/open-badges); [OB 3.0 implementation guide](https://www.imsglobal.org/spec/ob/v3p0/impl)
- **[STRONG, primary standard]** An **alignment** references an external or internal achievement/competency definition, e.g. a CASE competency-framework item. Implementation guides are informative, not normative. — [OB 3.0 implementation guide](https://www.imsglobal.org/spec/ob/v3p0/impl)
- **[PRACTITIONER, company self-report]** IBM's first Open Badges pilot (Big Data University) reported a 226% increase in course completion, a 694% increase in exam pass rates and a 64% increase in product trial downloads. — [Training Industry](https://trainingindustry.com/magazine/issue/open-badges-reimagining-the-credential-space-with-significant-unintended-benefits/)
- **[PRACTITIONER, company self-report]** IBM's own blog states the link between badges and sales quota attainment is correlational and that causation is undetermined; 92% of IBM survey respondents said badges improve their employability. — [IBM Training and Skills blog copy](https://www.d2l.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Do-digital-badges-really-provide-value-to-businesses_-IBM-Training-and-Skills-Blog.pdf); [Credly/IBM case study (vendor)](https://learn.credly.com/case-studies/credly-ibm-casestudy)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** Sodexo "Digital Passport": a gamified cloud-based programme to build employee digital skills, split into three learning "universes" of progressively deeper training, the first open to all employees. — [Sodexo](https://ca.sodexo.com/inspired-thinking/all-insights/big-or-agile-be-both.html)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** Global Trade Passport programme: each digital stamp represents 20 verified learning hours across webinars, certificates and similar activities. — [GTP Alliance](https://gtpalliance.com/global-trade-passport-program/digital-stamps)
- **[PRACTITIONER, vendor marketing]** Momentum Group (Adobe Learning Manager case study): social learning, badges and leaderboards used to drive adoption; unique logins reported rising from about 1,500 (Jan 2023) to 15,000 (Jan 2024). — [Adobe customer story](https://business.adobe.com/customer-success-stories/momentum-group-case-study.html)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** Other "passport" uses: a club-industry onboarding passport (CMAA Idea Fair entry, no outcome data); Purdue's "Passport" digital badging system; CCL Passport is a leadership content subscription, not a stamp book. — [CMAA](https://cmaa.org/idea-fair/idea-fair-entries/congo-passport-program); [Purdue](https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/archive/releases/2019/Q2/learning-passport-could-advance-education-into-the-digital-world.html); [CCL](https://www.ccl.org/passport-leadership-content-subscription/)

### Inferences
- Model each stamp as an Open Badges-style record even if the app never exports it: `name`, `description`, `criteria` (plain-language rule), `evidence` (system record or uploaded artifact), `issuer` (company academy, plus who verified), `issuedOn`, `alignment` (competency in the agent development framework), and optional `validUntil`. This makes every stamp auditable and defensible to compliance.
- Stackable pathway: stamps → page (quarter/stage) → passport level, mirroring Sodexo's tiered "universes" and the Global Trade Passport's "one stamp = fixed verified unit" rule.
- A stamp that anyone can get without evidence has no signalling value; issuer credibility comes from criteria plus verification, not from visual design.
- IBM's figures are useful as an illustration only; they are uncontrolled before/after self-reports.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or independent evaluation of a corporate badge or passport programme was found.
- The normative Open Badges 3.0 spec text was not read directly (only search extracts of the guides); field names should be checked against the spec before implementation.
- Whether Thai insurers or the Thai industry associations issue Open Badges-compliant credentials to agents: not found.

## 4. Leaderboards and competition in sales teams: risks and fairer designs

### Takeaway
Leaderboard evidence is genuinely mixed: rankings can raise output, but the one workplace field experiment on salespeople found mostly negative effects of showing ranks, and absolute public rankings tend to benefit only those near the top. Relative ("neighbours only") displays and hiding the bottom ranks are the best-supported mitigations, though the supporting studies are educational.

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE]** Barankay, "Rank Incentives" (Wharton): randomized workplace experiment where some salespeople were shown their rank and others were not, with pay unchanged; rankings affected sales, "but mostly in the wrong direction". — [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=15141)
- **[MODERATE]** Bai et al. (2021) tested top/middle/bottom positions on absolute and relative leaderboards: on the absolute board only the top third were more intrinsically motivated (without higher engagement or performance); on the relative board all learners were equally motivated and engaged. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10639-021-10770-2)
- **[MODERATE]** Reviews conclude that high leaderboard positions raise motivation while low positions can be detrimental, that absolute leaderboards risk discouraging lower-ranked participants, and that limiting public display of bottom ranks may help. — [Emerald review](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/intr-12-2021-0897/full/html); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10639-021-10770-2)
- **[MODERATE]** Contrary evidence exists: one study found extroverted and agreeable users' motivation did not drop significantly at the bottom, and another found low-ranked learners reported higher competitive effort and better retention than high-ranked ones. — [Jia et al. 2018](https://scholarworks.iupui.edu/bitstream/1805/17645/1/Jia_2018_designing.pdf); [Emerald review](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/intr-12-2021-0897/full/html)
- **[MODERATE]** Leaderboards are cited as raising performance in Landers et al. and Mekler et al., but in Mekler's study without gains in intrinsic motivation. — [Mekler et al. PDF](https://glassmanlab.seas.harvard.edu/annotated_works/mekler17.pdf); [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/gamification-of-task-performance-with-leaderboards-a-goal-setting/)
- **[MODERATE]** Sailer et al. (2017): teammates increased social relatedness. — [Augsburg repository](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109059)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** Duolingo leagues: weekly cohorts where top finishers are promoted to a harder league and low finishers demoted; commentary claims cohorts are segmented by engagement level to limit bottom-rank disengagement (not a documented finding). — [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/duolingo-streaks); [Deconstructor of Fun](https://www.deconstructoroffun.com/blog/2025/4/14/duolingo-how-the-15b-app-uses-gaming-principles-to-supercharge-dau-growth)
- **[STRONG, regulator]** The UK FSA/FCA lists "competitions" and "first past the post" bonuses among incentive features that increase mis-selling risk. — [Linklaters summary of FG13/1](https://www.linklaters.com/knowledge/publications/alerts-newsletters-and-guides/2013/january/fsa-final-guidance---risks-to-customers-from-financial-incentives); [Aon McLagan summary](https://mclagan.aon.com/aon.mclagan/media/files/2013/2013-Jan_UK-_FSA_guidance_on_incentives.pdf?ext=.pdf)

### Inferences
- Make the passport primarily **self-referenced** (my stamps vs my plan, my personal best, my cohort's median) and treat any ranking as optional and secondary.
- If a leaderboard is included: rank on *development* stamps and quality-gated milestones, not raw premium; show only the agent's neighbours (relative view); cohort agents by tenure (same intake month/quarter) so month-3 agents are not compared with veterans; hide or anonymise the bottom portion; prefer unit/team progress boards.
- A sales-volume leaderboard for licensed insurance agents is a conduct risk as well as a motivation risk, because regulators treat competitions as a mis-selling risk feature.

### Gaps
- The "macro vs micro leaderboard" terminology and Duolingo's actual league results were not found in primary sources.
- Research on sales contest prize structure (e.g. Lim, Ahearne & Ham on multiple-winner formats) was searched for and **not found**; no claim is made about it.
- Barankay's effect sizes and setting details were not retrieved (press summary only).
- No leaderboard study on insurance agents or on Asian sales forces was found; cultural moderators (e.g. face, collectivism) are unaddressed by the sources retrieved.

## 5. Failure modes of sales contests / gamified training, mis-selling regulation, and safeguards

### Takeaway
The dominant failure mode is metric gaming and mis-selling when rewards attach to sales counts through thresholds, accelerators and competitions; regulators in the UK, Singapore and at IAIS level explicitly require reward structures to reflect quality and fair customer outcomes. A stamp system for agents should therefore gate every sales-linked milestone on quality and compliance measures and avoid cliff-edge sales targets.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG, regulator]** FSA Final Guidance FG13/1 "Risks to customers from financial incentives" (January 2013): Section 3 covers scheme features that increase mis-selling risk and Section 4 covers managing risks and governance. Features flagged include thresholds (pay only above a minimum sales level), accelerators or stepped payments (higher rates above target), competitions, and disproportionate rewards for marginal sales such as cross-selling multipliers or "first past the post" bonuses. — [FCA FG13/1 PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fsa-fg13-01.pdf); [Linklaters summary](https://www.linklaters.com/knowledge/publications/alerts-newsletters-and-guides/2013/january/fsa-final-guidance---risks-to-customers-from-financial-incentives)
- **[STRONG, regulator]** Remedies suggested by the FSA: remove high-risk features and conflicts of interest (e.g. sales managers paid a share of their staff's commission), and reduce or cap sales-linked incentives. Firms must also manage sales targets and performance management so that the risk does not simply move there (later guidance FG15/10). — [FCA: risks to customers from financial incentives](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/risks-customers-financial-incentives); [FCA FG15/10](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/finalised-guidance/fg15-10-risks-customers-performance-management-firms)
- **[STRONG, regulator]** FCA's 2014 follow-up (TR14/4) found firms were not properly identifying how their schemes might encourage mis-selling; a later FCA review recognised that pay which is effectively 100% variable and sales-based increases mis-selling risk. — [FCA TR14/4](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/thematic-reviews/tr14-4-risks-customers-financial-incentives-update); [FCA press release](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/the-fca-publishes-latest-review-of-sales-incentives-at-retail-financial-services-firms)
- **[STRONG, regulator]** IAIS ICP 19 (Conduct of Business): insurers and intermediaries must treat customers fairly; "Reward structures need to reflect quality issues and not encourage or reward the unfair treatment of customers. Remuneration structures that create conflicts of interest may lead to poor customer outcomes." — [IAIS ICP 19 online tool](https://iaisweb.org/icp-online-tool/13530-icp-19-conduct-of-business); [IAIS ICPs and ComFrame, Dec 2024](https://www.iais.org/uploads/2025/06/IAIS-ICPs-and-ComFrame-December-2024.pdf)
- **[STRONG, regulator]** MAS (Singapore) Notice FAA-N20 sets a balanced-scorecard remuneration framework: a "specified variable income" portion of a representative's variable income is measured against **non-sales KPIs** (Annex 1), graded via a representatives' grading table, with recovery of income not earned; it also provides for an Independent Sales Audit Unit and covers supervisors. Last revised 29 December 2025. — [MAS Notice FAA-N20](https://www.mas.gov.sg/regulation/notices/notice-faa-n20); [MAS BSC FAQs](https://www.mas.gov.sg/regulation/faqs/faqs-on-the-balanced-scorecard-bsc-framework)
- **[MODERATE, third-party summary]** The four non-sales KPIs commonly cited for the MAS framework: understanding the client's needs, suitability of product recommendations, adequacy of information disclosure, professional and ethical conduct. — [Waystone compliance summary](https://compliance.waystone.com/financial-advisors-balanced-scorecard-requirements/)
- **[MODERATE, law-firm summaries]** Thailand OIC: the 2018-2020 framework on offering insurance requires verifying the seller's identity and authority, disclosing the purpose of contact and giving complete and accurate information; unit-linked/universal life sellers need specific training and registration. On 15 September 2026 the OIC issued notifications (life and non-life) amending the 2020 framework on policy offering, agent/broker conduct, premium collection and advertising, effective 1 January 2027, including submitting applications to the insurer no later than the next business day and informing customers of the right to cancel. — [Tilleke & Gibbins, 2026 amendments](https://www.tilleke.com/insights/thailand-amends-rules-on-insurance-policy-delivery-sales-and-advertising); [Tilleke & Gibbins, 2019 regulations](https://www.tilleke.com/insights/thailands-oic-issues-new-regulations-providing-insurance-effective-january-1-2019); [Baker McKenzie 2018](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/-/media/files/insight/publications/2018/06/al_bangkok_oicimprovesregulation_jun18.pdf?la=en)
- **[STRONG, case record]** Wells Fargo: employees opened unauthorised accounts to meet cross-selling sales goals (CFPB fine of US$100 million); some managers checked progress against goals twice a day; ethics training in 2014 did not fix it because the incentive structure was unchanged; product sales goals were ended effective 1 January 2017; turnover of up to 42% a year was not connected by HR to unmet goals. — [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/blog/hundreds-thousands-accounts-secretly-created-wells-fargo-bank-employees-leads-historic-100-million-fine-cfpb); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wells_Fargo_cross-selling_scandal); [SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/in/topics-tools/employment-law-compliance/regulator-criticizes-wells-fargos-hr-wake-phony-accounts)
- **[MODERATE]** Ordóñez, Schweitzer, Galinsky & Bazerman, "Goals Gone Wild" (Academy of Management Perspectives 23(1), 2009): systematic side effects of goal setting include narrow focus that neglects non-goal areas, distorted risk preferences, more unethical behaviour, inhibited learning, corroded culture and reduced intrinsic motivation; unethical behaviour rises particularly when people fall just short of a goal (Schweitzer, Ordóñez & Douma 2004). — [Harvard Kennedy School](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/goals-gone-wild-systematic-side-effects-overprescribing-goal-setting); [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/when-goal-setting-goes-bad)
- **[STRONG]** Larkin (2014, Journal of Labor Economics 32(2), 199-227): at a leading enterprise software vendor, a non-linear (accelerating) commission scheme led salespeople to game deal timing and produced pricing distortions. — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jlabec:doi:10.1086/673371)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** A Carnegie Mellon (Tepper) news piece reports research arguing workplace gamification can erode moral agency. — [CMU Tepper](https://www.cmu.edu/tepper/news/stories/workplace-gamification-erodes-moral-agency)

### Inferences
- **Stamp design that mirrors regulator concerns:** no stamp should be earnable by sales count alone. Sales-linked stamps should require a quality gate that matures later, e.g. policies still in force after a persistency window, clean needs-analysis/suitability file, no upheld complaint, no free-look cancellation spike.
- **Avoid cliff edges and deadlines on sales stamps.** "Goals Gone Wild" and Larkin both point to misconduct and timing games concentrating near thresholds and period ends. Prefer rolling windows and stamps that can be earned later without penalty; monitor for spikes of activity just before a stamp deadline.
- **Balance the book** in the spirit of the MAS scorecard: a fixed share of stamps for knowledge, skills/behaviours, compliance/ethics and client outcomes, with production stamps a minority. A compliance breach should suspend or revoke related stamps (clawback analogue).
- **Manager verification with a conflict check:** managers who earn overrides on their agents' sales have a conflict (flagged by the FSA), so skill-observation stamps should use a rubric, be sample-audited, and be visible to an independent training/compliance function.
- **Short-termism:** a 24-month passport is itself a counterweight if late-stage stamps reward durable outcomes (persistency, client reviews, referrals) rather than first-year volume.
- UK and Singapore texts are not binding in Thailand; they are used here as the most explicit published statements of what supervisors regard as risky.

### Gaps
- The FG13/1 PDF could not be opened (host blocked); feature lists come from law-firm/consultancy summaries and FCA page extracts. Whether it names "retrospective accelerators" was not confirmed.
- **No Thai OIC text was found that addresses sales incentives, contests or persistency requirements for agents.** OIC full notification texts were not retrieved; the 2026 amendment details rest on one law firm's summary and should be checked against the official notification.
- ICP 19 guidance paragraph numbers and exact wording beyond the quoted sentences were not retrieved.
- Larkin's quantified cost estimates were not retrieved.
- No published empirical evaluation of quality gates (e.g. persistency-conditioned rewards) on agent behaviour was found.

## 7. Synthesis: concrete design rules for a 24-month stamp-collecting milestone system

### Takeaway
The evidence supports a self-referenced, evidence-backed, quality-gated passport divided into short pages, with competition and tangible rewards kept secondary. Every rule below is an inference from sections 1-6, graded by the strength of the evidence behind it.

### Cited Findings
- All supporting findings are cited in sections 1-6; the most load-bearing are: endowed progress 34% vs 19% completion — [Nunes & Drèze](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2006i4p504-512.html); goal-gradient acceleration — [Kivetz et al.](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal); small positive learning effects with story and social moderators — [Sailer & Homner](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w); incentives predict quantity, intrinsic motivation predicts quality — [Cerasoli et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24491020/); reward structures must reflect quality — [IAIS ICP 19](https://iaisweb.org/icp-online-tool/13530-icp-19-conduct-of-business); thresholds, accelerators and competitions raise mis-selling risk — [FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/risks-customers-financial-incentives); broken streaks demotivate unless repairable — [Silverman & Barasch](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman); badge data model — [Open Badges 3.0](https://www.imsglobal.org/spec/ob/v3p0/impl).

### Inferences
Rules backed by strong evidence:
1. **Start the book part-filled.** Pre-stamp real prior achievements (licence, onboarding) with the reason stated.
2. **Short pages, visible distance-to-goal.** Roughly quarterly pages of a handful of stamps each, with "x of y" progress, so the agent is usually close to completing something.
3. **Specific, challenging, verifiable criteria per stamp**, with feedback on what is missing.
4. **No stamp on sales volume alone; no thresholds, accelerators or prize competitions tied to sales stamps.** Attach persistency/suitability/complaint gates and allow revocation.
5. **Keep rewards mostly symbolic and informational.** Recognition, status, unlocks of learning or mentoring; avoid cash-equivalent prizes per stamp.

Rules backed by moderate evidence:
6. **Wrap the journey in a story and make it social** (cohort/unit pages, mentor sign-off, team stamps).
7. **Self-referenced first; leaderboards optional, relative, tenure-cohorted, bottom hidden, never on raw premium.**
8. **Streaks only for low-stakes learning habits, repairable, with no "streak lost" notifications.**
9. **Evidence and issuer on every stamp** (Open Badges-style fields; manager verification with rubric and audit sampling).
10. **Plan for novelty decay:** new chapters, changing challenge types and stage-appropriate goals across months 1-6, 7-12, 13-24 (learning/behaviour goals early, durable client-outcome goals late).

Practitioner-level / untested:
11. Local language and culturally familiar framing.
12. Measure it: define leading indicators (stamp completion by cohort, time between stamps) and lagging quality indicators (persistency, complaints, 12/24-month agent retention), and compare cohorts.

### Gaps
- None of these rules has been tested as a package on insurance agents; optimal number of stamps per page, page length and reward value are not specified by any source found.
- No evidence was found on how gamified development systems interact with existing agent compensation and contest calendars, which will likely dominate agent behaviour.
